\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Long belt conveyors are elastic systems. When the drive starts or stops a conveyor, the
change of speed travels along the belt as a stress wave, at \qtyrange{1.5}{2}{\kilo\metre\per\second} on an empty steel-cord belt and much more slowly on a
loaded carry strand, so that in a conveyor several kilometres long the belt far from the
drive starts moving seconds after the belt at the drive
\citep{harrison1983,nordell1984,lodewijks2002}. The transient tensions that result govern
the belt rating, the travel of the take-up and the grip of the belt on the drive pulleys,
and dynamic analysis has become part of the design of long overland conveyors
\citep{lodewijks2002,nordell1997zisco}. Most of these conveyors are tensioned by a gravity
take-up: a counterweight that hangs from a carriage whose pulley is wrapped by the return
belt. It holds the slack-side tension in running and stores the belt that the loop releases
or takes up as its stretch changes.

Where to place the take-up is a design decision, and practice takes it partly on dynamic
grounds. The usual position, right after a head drive, keeps the tension of the belt leaving
the drive constant and minimises the chance of slip \citep{lodewijks2013}. On a
\qty{5.1}{\kilo\metre} conveyor, \citet{harrison1983} found the slow oscillation of the belt
speed after a start to be synchronous with the motion of the take-up and expected it to be
weaker with the take-up at the tail; he later listed a take-up at the tail, or at the point
of lowest tension, among the remedies for the surge of the return belt during start-up
\citep{harrison1985b}. On the \qty{15.6}{\kilo\metre} ZISCO conveyor the take-up was placed
after the tail drive, close to a horizontal curve, because a take-up at the head station
would have produced much larger tension fluctuations in the curve \citep{nordell1997zisco};
a \qty{21}{\kilo\metre} conveyor with head and tail drives has its gravity take-up at the
tail \citep{kruse2019overland}; and a simulated drift stop of a \qty{1}{\kilo\metre}
incline left a minimum tension of \qty{3.5}{\kilo\newton} with the take-up at the head
against \qty{20.4}{\kilo\newton} at the tail \citep{cdi2002}. Owners have also replaced
gravity take-ups by fixed ones, or moved them ``in the hope of reducing observed hazards''
\citep{nordell1987}. In the words of
\citet{cornet2002}, the location of the take-up is dictated by the terrain and ``by the belt
dynamic behavior''. In each of these cases the effect of the position is established for
one conveyor, by simulation or by experience; a general, quantitative description of how
the take-up position shapes the longitudinal dynamics is not available.

The ingredients of such a description are known separately. The two wave speeds of the
return and carry strands, and their dependence on how firmly the bulk material is coupled
to the belt, are well established \citep{nordell1984,lodewijks2002,cdi1998}. The take-up
is represented in three ways. Discrete and finite-element models of the whole loop include
the kinematics of the take-up pulley, which stores twice its own travel of belt
\citep{lodewijks1996,kulinowski2014,song2003}; they answer the question for a given layout,
one simulation at a time, and in \citet{lodewijks1996} the take-up is merged with the drive
into a single moving pulley. Equivalent models replace the take-up by a mass embedded in
the belt \citep[Fig.~2]{harrison1983} or shared between the belt elements next to it
\citep{sakharwade2019}, which gives the wrong limits for a light and for a heavy take-up
(Section~\ref{sec:interfaces}). Continuum models, which could give the dependence in closed
form, have so far set the take-up aside. \Citet{song2012} wrote the two-strand continuum
with the take-up condition, but neglected the take-up inertia and moved the take-up from the
head drive, where their conveyor has it, to the tail, where the two strands separate into
two quarter-wave problems. \Citet{pang2015} solved a single viscoelastic span clamped at one end
and carrying a concentrated mass at the other, a mass that shares the rigid-body
acceleration of the belt; \citet{li2018} applied the same formulation to a tail take-up,
with half of the take-up mass at the end of each strand. \Citet{ellis1987} replaced the
counterweight by a constant tension at the tail of the carry strand and left out the return
strand, and \citet{pihnastyi2020} prescribe the tensions at both ends of the carry strand.
In a recent discrete study the counterweight mass was varied together with the static
tension it produces \citep{gao2026}, so that the inertia of the take-up and the pre-tension
of the belt changed at once.

The common element is elementary in wave mechanics, and it is the starting point of this
paper: a gravity take-up whose inertial impedance is small compared with that of the belt
is a free end. It holds a constant force, so a stretching front that reaches it is
reflected as at a free end and almost nothing is transmitted to the belt beyond it. The
loop then splits at the take-up into two strands, each fixed at the drive and free at the
take-up, whose lengths are set by the take-up position. Finite-element simulations of
the 1980s showed the reflection: once the counterweight moves, disturbances that reach it
are ``primarily reflected'' \citep{morrison1987}, and the tensile wave of the return strand
in a stop is ``absorbed by the take-up'' \citep{nordell1987}. \Citet{harrison1985b}
described the consequence in his records, in which the return belt beyond the take-up did
not move until the stress had travelled round the whole belt. Discrete models reproduce the
effect case by case, but none of the models above builds it into its structure: they move
the take-up to the tail, embed it, merge it with the drive or attach its mass to the
belt. Built into a closed-form characteristic equation of the loop, the free end
accounts for three observations that have so far been unrelated. It explains why the slow
take-up oscillation that Harrison measured, which he attributed to the mass of the
counterweight oscillating on the stiffness of the belt, hardly depends on that mass; why moving the
take-up from the head to the tail is not a neutral simplification, since it changes the
fundamental period of the conveyor of \citet{song2012} from
\qty{\NumSongPeriodHeadLight}{\second} to \qty{\NumSongPeriodTailLight}{\second}; and why
the return belt downstream of the take-up stays at rest for one wave transit. The aim is
therefore not to show that the take-up position matters, which practice knows, but to
quantify its effect in closed, dimensionless form.

The free end belongs to long conveyors, and the analysis gives the term a definition.
``Long-distance conveyor'' has no strict definition in the technical literature
\citep{suchorab2025longdistance}. A gravity take-up holds its tension with its own weight,
so its mass is tied to the tension it provides, and the ratio of the take-up mass to the
mass of the return strand equals, up to a factor set by the rigging, the ratio of the take-up
tension to the weight of the return strand. Since the take-up tension is sized by requirements that grow
with the length, this ratio does not fall as the conveyor gets longer. We call a conveyor
long, for its longitudinal dynamics, when the take-up tension is small against the weight
of the return strand, so that the take-up acts as a free end and its mass enters only as a
correction (Section~\ref{sec:betaregime}).

The contributions of the paper are the following.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A closed-form characteristic equation for the full loop, with two wave speeds, a
  gravity take-up anywhere on the return strand modelled as a pulley with mass and the drive
  anywhere on the loop (Section~\ref{sec:solution}). Its natural frequencies interlace with
  those of the two strands with the take-up free, and in the limit of a light take-up the
  loop splits into these two fixed--free strands, for free and forced vibration alike.
  \item A closed-form correction for the take-up mass, which acts as a tip mass of one
  quarter of its value on each strand, and an explicit threshold beyond which the mass
  matters (Sections~\ref{sec:masscorrection} and~\ref{sec:betaregime}). With the mass ratio
  written in terms of the take-up tension, the take-up mass lengthens the fundamental period
  of every conveyor longer than \qty{1}{\kilo\metre} in the literature by at most
  \qty{\NumLongShiftMax}{\percent}, and by at most \qty{\NumLongShiftMaxLow}{\percent} over
  the coupling range of each material. For a counterweight hung directly near the drive and a
  carry strand at least 1.4 times slower than the return strand, the effect stays below
  \qty{5}{\percent} as long as the take-up tension is below a quarter of the weight of the
  return strand.
  \item The fundamental mode belongs to the strand that contains the carry strand, with
  closed-form bounds on its period (Section~\ref{sec:frequencies}). Moving the take-up from
  the head to the tail shortens the fundamental period by a factor of up to
  \NumHeadTailRatioGammaOne\ for equal wave speeds and \NumHeadTailRatioGammaTwo\ for a
  carry strand twice as slow, and a drive on the return strand lengthens it by up to
  \qty{\NumIntermediateOverMax}{\percent} beyond four transits of that strand for wave-speed
  ratios up to 3.
  \item A universal start-up curve of the fixed--free strand, from which the peak tensions at
  both faces of the drive and the take-up travel follow in closed form, a start-time rule
  in units of the fundamental period, and explicit conditions on slack, take-up motion and
  drive grip (Sections~\ref{sec:startup} and~\ref{sec:validity}).
  \item Verification against an independent lumped-mass model, consistency checks against
  the field records that bear on the take-up, and an application to three conveyors from the
  literature (Sections~\ref{sec:validation} and~\ref{sec:application}), in which the
  take-up position turns out to act mainly through the static tensions, and the grip of a
  single drive pulley, rather than the dynamic amplification, fixes the start time.
\end{enumerate}

Section~\ref{sec:model} states the model and Section~\ref{sec:solution} its closed-form
solution. Section~\ref{sec:validation} verifies and checks it, Section~\ref{sec:results}
gives the results for natural frequencies, take-up mass, start-up and validity, and
Section~\ref{sec:application} applies them to three conveyors.
Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses the relation to practice and the limitations, and
Section~\ref{sec:conclusions} concludes.
